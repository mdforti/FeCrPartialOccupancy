\begin{abstract}
The \textsigma-phase is a complex intermetallic that precipitates in a wide range of metallic materials when subject to high temperatures.
As it often produces embrittlement of the alloy, understanding its properties and conditions of stability is of key importance in alloy development. 
The Fe–Cr \textsigma-phase forms in alloys with about 45–50\,at.\% Cr at 600\,°C. 
Atomistic studies usually assume full sublattice occupation or complete disorder, but experiments show that several Wyckoff sites exhibit mixed occupancies, creating many possible local atomic configurations.
This configurational complexity limits direct first-principles studies and leaves open questions about how site occupancy influences the elastic properties of the \textsigma-phase.

We address this problem using a combination of density functional theory and machine learning interatomic potentials for a Fe\textsubscript{16}Cr\textsubscript{14} composition that matches experimental site occupancies.
Formation enthalpies are referenced to spin-polarized (FM) BCC Fe and Cr ground states throughout, providing a physically consistent energy scale for both spin-polarized and non-spin-polarized calculations.
We screen all possible configurations of the disordered intermetallic based on formation energy and identify the minimum-energy partial-occupancy configuration ($C^{PO,\min}$), which represents the physically most consistent atomistic estimate of the elastic tensor.
This configuration is validated against DFT calculations and used as the reference to assess the accuracy of the classical full-occupancy sublattice (FO) and compound energy formalism (CEF) approximations.

Our results show that partial occupancy stabilizes the \textsigma-phase and lowers the unit-cell symmetry from tetragonal to triclinic.
The elastic constants predicted by the FO and CEF approximations deviate by roughly 10\% from the minimum-energy PO reference, with both approximations systematically overestimating stiffness.
These findings provide a clearer picture of how local chemical disorder affects the mechanical response of the \textsigma-phase and highlight the importance of including explicit configurational effects when modeling complex intermetallic compounds.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The \textsigma-phase is a topologically close-packed intermetallic that precipitates at elevated temperatures in multiple alloy families and is frequently associated with degraded structural performance, especially embrittlement and reduced service tolerance in critical composition-temperature windows.
In Fe-Cr-based materials, this issue is particularly important because \textsigma~can form in technologically relevant processing and exposure conditions, and its presence can alter both thermophysical and mechanical behavior \cite{lit_1958_08_bungardt_archiv_fuer_das_eisenhuettenwesen,lit_2008_03_joubert_progress_in_materials_science,lit_2009_07_gorbunoff_journal_of_alloys_and_compounds,lit_2019_09_hajra_intermetallics,lit_2023_05_kishida_acta_materialia}.

At the crystal-chemistry level, the Fe-Cr \textsigma-phase is usually represented as tetragonal (space group \textit{P4\textsubscript{2}/mnm}) with five nonequivalent Wyckoff sublattices. 
However, experimental and modeling evidence indicates that mixed site occupancy and local compositional fluctuations are intrinsic to realistic \textsigma~states, and these effects can influence magnetic response, local bonding, and therefore elastic and deformation behavior \cite{lit_2012_09_kabliman_applied_sciences,lit_2017_01_cieslak_acta_materialia,lit_2023_05_kishida_acta_materialia}. 
This makes Fe-Cr \textsigma~a prototypical system where long-range crystallographic symmetry and local chemical disorder must be treated simultaneously.

A substantial part of the literature has addressed \textsigma-phase properties through sublattice or mean-field-style formalisms, frequently combined with first-principles calculations of ordered configurations and thermodynamic interpolation.
These approaches have provided major advances and remain essential for tractable composition-temperature mapping, including CEF/CALPHAD-based descriptions \cite{lit_2008_03_joubert_progress_in_materials_science,lit_2012_09_kabliman_applied_sciences,lit_2023_12_hallstedt_calphad}. 
Related first-principles studies in \textsigma-containing systems further show that elastic response and stability are highly sensitive to chemistry and local arrangement \cite{lit_2017_02_bonny_scripta_materialia,lit_2015_02_li_computational_materials_science,lit_2023_04_vesti_journal_of_nuclear_materials}.
Nevertheless, explicitly enumerating the distinct local occupancy configurations across the five nonequivalent sublattices produces a combinatorial explosion of possible arrangements that grows rapidly with system size and composition, making exhaustive first-principles treatment prohibitive and historically driving the use of symmetry-preserving or mean-field approximations.
The unresolved question is therefore the extent to which this explicit local occupancy disorder modifies elastic tensors relative to those sublattice approximations in the Fe-Cr \textsigma~ phase itself.

Recent developments in data-driven alloy modeling demonstrate that machine-learning-assisted workflows can accelerate screening of large configuration spaces while preserving useful physical trends when anchored to first-principles data \cite{lit_2022_06_vazquez_acta_materialia,lit_2025_11_zhang_acta_materialia,lit_2025_01_bijjala_acta_materialia}.
In parallel, current work on complex metallic crystals and TCP formation pathways emphasizes that local environments and defect-level mechanisms can substantially affect emergent properties \cite{lit_2022_04_kishida_acta_materialia,lit_2022_12_turner_nature_communications,lit_2024_08_bai_acta_materialia}.
Together, these results motivate a strategy where explicit configurational sampling and targeted DFT validation are combined rather than replaced by averaged descriptions.

In this work, we keep the focus on the same scientific goal: quantifying how partial occupancy affects the elastic response of Fe-Cr \textsigma-phase at nominal Fe\textsubscript{16}Cr\textsubscript{14} composition.
We compare classical full-occupancy/sublattice representations with models that include explicit local mixed occupancy, and we evaluate the resulting changes in stability, symmetry, and elastic tensors using a combined DFT and MLIP workflow.
This preserves the original objective while situating it explicitly within the broader \textsigma-phase literature.

The contribution of this study relative to prior work is therefore threefold.
First, it provides a direct Fe-Cr \textsigma assessment of how explicit local occupancy modifies mechanical descriptors beyond mean-field occupancy averaging.
Second, it links configurational disorder to symmetry reduction and elastic-tensor variation in a way directly comparable to existing sublattice and CEF practice.
Third, it complements ongoing experimental and computational \textsigma literature by offering a practical path to reconcile physically interpretable thermodynamic models with configuration-resolved atomistic mechanics \cite{lit_2008_03_joubert_progress_in_materials_science,lit_2012_09_kabliman_applied_sciences,lit_2023_05_kishida_acta_materialia,lit_2023_12_hallstedt_calphad,lit_2025_11_zhang_acta_materialia}.

\section{Methodology}

We employ first-principles DFT calculations to determine the elastic constants of the Fe-Cr \textsigma-phase. 
First, we study the change of elastic constants with composition under a full site occupancy approximation.
Then, we focus on a nominal composition of Fe\textsubscript{16}Cr\textsubscript{14} (48\%at. Cr) to study the effect of partial occupancy explicitly.
These calculations are conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) ~\cite{Hafner_vasp}, utilizing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) ~\cite{Perdew1996} exchange-correlation functional and the Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) method ~\cite{Bloch1994, kresse_ultrasoft_1999}.
\paragraph{Magnetic ordering}
The Fe–Cr \textsigma-phase is magnetic below a Curie temperature of approximately 106\,K \cite{nevitt1955curie}.
At the service temperatures relevant to alloy embrittlement (above 870\,K) the phase is therefore non-magnetic.
Kabliman et al.\ \cite{kabliman2009first} showed that the 0\,K ground state is ferrimagnetic, with Fe and Cr sublattices ferromagnetically ordered among themselves but antiferromagnetically coupled to each other.
Later work by the same group \cite{KablimanConfigurationalThermoSigma} demonstrated that magnetic entropy has a noticeable influence on the site-preference of Fe and Cr atoms but leaves the overall phase behaviour qualitatively unchanged above T$_C$.
Accordingly, we perform non-spin-polarized (NM) calculations for the determination of the elastic constants, consistent with the paramagnetic state at service temperatures.
An estimation of the errors introduced by this approximation is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Formation enthalpies throughout this work are referenced to the spin-polarized (FM) BCC ground states of elemental Fe and Cr.
This choice is physically motivated: FM BCC is the true 0\,K ground state of both elements and provides a consistent, well-defined reference for comparing NM and FM \textsigma-phase configurations on a common energy scale.

\paragraph{Full occupancy}\label{methodology:fo} First, we calculate the properties of the \textsigma-phase using a sublattice model.
This means, that the lattice positions of a WS are occupied either by Cr or Fe.
This way we calculate  2\textsuperscript{5} = 32 ordered structures. 
These so-called end-member configurations can be used to find the optimal site occupancy and Gibbs free energy at any finite temperature for any nominal Fe (or Cr) composition $x$ using the compound energy formalism (CEF) ~\cite{dupin_using_2006, crivello_first_2009, zhang_prediction_2025}.
Here, the occupancy of an element $i$ in a sublattice $s$ is defined in terms of the number of atoms $N_i ^s$ in the sublattice,

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:occupancy}
  y_i ^s = \dfrac{N_i ^s}{\sum _j N_j ^s}
\end{equation}

\noindent and can be calculated from an optimization problem which minimizes the weighted Gibbs free energy \cite{zhang_prediction_2025}. 
In this approach, the Gibbs free energy is usually derived from a Gorsky-Bragg-Williams approximation without excess terms\cite{gorsky_rontgenographische_1928,bragg_effect_1997,chizhikov_gorsky-bragg-williams_2000}.
In particular for \textsigma-phase,

\begin{align}\label{eqn:CEF}
  \begin{split}
    \Delta G(x, T) &= \Delta H(x, T) - T S(x, T) \\
		   &= \min _{ y_i ^{2a}  \dots y_m ^{8i} } 
		   \left[  
		     \sum_{i j k l m = Fe, Cr} {}
		   \left[  
                    y_i^{2a} y_j^{4f} y_k^{8i_1} y_l ^{8i_2} y_m ^{8j}
                   \right] H_{i j k l m} ^0 
		   + k_B T \sum_s \dfrac{m_s}{N} \sum_i y_i ^s \ln y_i ^s
	           \right]
  \end{split}
\end{align}

\noindent where the indexes $i, j, k, l, m$ run over the elements Fe and Cr for for the sublattices \siteA, \siteB,  \siteC, \siteD~and \siteE~respectively.
\MFnew{
In eq 2, the temperature dependence of the enthalpy variation arises from that of the site occupancies (e.g., $y_i ^{2a}$), while $H^0_{ijklm}$ is calculated at 0K and temperature independent.
}
The compound index \textit{\ijklm} identifies a particular end member with a given configuration. 
$H_{i j k l m} ^0$ are the formation enthalpies at 0K of such configuraition, $m_s$ are the multiplicities of the sublattices \siteA \ldots \siteE~with $m_s = 2, 4, 8, 8, 8$ respectively, and $N$ is the total number of atoms in the unit cell.
As this is a well established method, we refer to the original literature for further details.
We calculate the formation enthalpies $\Delta H_{ijklm}^0$ in reference to the spin-polarized (FM) BCC ground states of Fe and Cr, 

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:formation_enthalpy}
  \Delta H_{\ijklm}^0 = E_{\ijklm}^{\sigma} - \left( x_{\ijklm}^{Fe} E^{Fe,FM}_{0,\text{bcc}} + x_{\ijklm}^{Cr} E^{Cr,FM}_{0,\text{bcc}} \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E_{\ijklm}^{\sigma}$ is the total energy of one unit cell of the \textsigma-phase, $E_{0,\text{bcc}}^{Fe,FM}$ and $E_{0,\text{bcc}}^{Cr,FM}$ are the energies per atom of the spin-polarized BCC ground states of Fe and Cr, respectively, and $x_{\ijklm}^{Fe}$ and $x_{ijklm}^{Cr}$ are the atomic fractions of Fe and Cr in the \textsigma-phase unit cell for the specific configuration.
Using FM BCC references ensures that both NM and FM \textsigma-phase calculations are evaluated against the same, physically correct elemental ground states.


\paragraph{Partial occupancy}\label{methodology:po} To capture the impact of atomic disorder, we explicitly explore all possible atomic arrangements within the \textsigma-phase unit cell, incorporating experimentally reported partial occupancy of WS by Yakel~\cite{yakel_atom_1983} as reproduced in \autoref{fig:PartialOccupancies}.  
A random distribution in the different sublattices could be modelled for instance by a special quasirandom structure \MFnew{[SQS ref]} but  this would require a big unit cell to generate a meaningful representation of such atomic distributions, in particular due to the small Cr content in the  2\textit{a} site (< 0.5 atom per unit cell).
\MFnew{
  In principle, partial occupancy could also be modelled from Monte Carlo molecular dynamics simulation (MDMC). 
  First, such an approach would also require big unit cells for sufficient statitstics to represent the partial occupancies accurately. 
  Secondly, the size of those unit cells would also make it difficult to explore elastic constants from DFT. 
  Alternative approaches like MLIPs could be used to speed up the calculations. Such an alternative will be explored in future works. 

}

In order to keep the size of the simulation to one unit cell, we consider two approximations to the partial occupancy, in both cases disregarding Cr occupation in site \siteA~and rounding occupancy to integer numbers.  
A first model keeps full occupancy of Fe in the sites \siteA~and \siteD, full Cr occupancy in site \siteB, but sites \siteC~and \siteE~will have 5 Cr and 3 Fe atoms per unit cell.
We name this model PO1. 
In this condition, the total number of possible configurations is $1 \times 1 \times \binom{8}{3} \times 1 \times \binom{8}{3} = 3136 $, all with the same nominal composition and site occupancy.

A second model further transfers one Cr atom from \siteB~to \siteD.
We call this other model PO2.
The total number of possible configurations now explodes to $ 1 \times 4 \times \binom{8}{3}\times \binom{8}{1} \times \binom{8}{3} \approx 100k $.
We choose the explained naming convention according to increasing number of possible configurations.
In both models, the combinatorial explosion of the number of configurations hinders any extensive estimation of the elastic constants of a significant number of samples from DFT calculations, although in the second model the number of configurations is significantly reduced and approaching statistical significance would be easier.

In comparison to these partial occupancy models, a sublattice model of the nominal composition of interest would have a very different site occupancy. 
As shown in \autoref{fig:PartialOccupancies}, there are three possible configurations with nominal composition Fe\textsubscript{16}Cr\textsubscript{14} ($\approx$ 52\,at.\% Fe). 
All of them have site \siteA~filled with Cr instead of Fe. 
In addition, only one of the \siteC, \siteD, and \siteE~sites would carry Cr, which may substantially destabilize the unit cell relative to partial-occupancy models.

Among all configurations explored within each partial-occupancy model, we identify the configuration with the lowest formation enthalpy as the minimum-energy partial-occupancy sample, denoted $C^{PO,\min}$.
This configuration represents the thermodynamically most favored local arrangement accessible within the partial-occupancy constraint, and therefore provides the physically most consistent estimate of the elastic tensor.
We use $C^{PO,\min}$ as the primary reference when quantifying how well the classical sublattice approximations (FO and CEF) reproduce the elastic properties of the σ-phase.

\paragraph{Elastic constants}

By designing deformation tensors which exploit the symmetry of the unit cell, the elastic tensor components can be determined from a parametrized strain-energy relation \cite{golesorkhtabar_elastic_2013}.

Under the sublattice model, it is also customary to approximate any property of the disordered structure as a weighted average of the properties of the end-member configurations~\cite{dupin_using_2006}.
In particular for the elastic constants $c_{IJ}$, 

\begin{equation}\label{cefcij}
  c_{IJ}^{CEF} (x, T) = \sum_{i j k l m = Fe, Cr} {}
  \left[  
    y_i^{2a} y_j^{4f} y_k^{8i_1} y_l ^{8i_2} y_m ^{8j}
\right] c_{IJ} ^{i j k l m}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{MLIP}

In recent years, universal machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) fitted on large DFT databases have emerged as efficient screening tools for complex configurational spaces~\cite{lit_2022_06_vazquez_acta_materialia,lit_2025_01_bijjala_acta_materialia}.
While such universal potentials can represent a wide range of chemistries, the complex atomic environments and elastic response of the \textsigma-phase may challenge their accuracy.
Here we instead fit a dedicated GRACE\MFnew{missing ref} potential from scratch on a targeted Fe-Cr \textsigma-phase DFT dataset, using it solely as a screening tool to rank the large set of partial-occupancy configurations by formation enthalpy, not as a replacement for DFT elastic constant calculations.
As a first step, we select a subset of 25 representative configurations of each model and calculate their optimized unit cell and their elastic constants using DFT. 
We extend the resulting dataset to build a specialized Fe-Cr \textsigma-phase DFT training set consisting of 

\begin{itemize}
	\item the relaxation paths of all full-occupancy samples,
	\item the relaxation paths of 50 ad-hoc chosen partial-occupancy samples,
	\item random deformations including isotropic compression, expansion, and atom-position shaking, 
	\item relaxation paths for unit cell optimization of the BCC, FCC, and HCP unary structures of Fe and Cr, 
	\item nearest-neighbour distance expansions of the BCC, FCC, and HCP unary structures of Fe and Cr.
\end{itemize}

All available DFT data are used to fit the GRACE potential from scratch.



\section{Results}\label{sec:Results}

In the sublattice model, the Fe-Cr~\textsigma--phase exhibits a tetragonal unit cell in the space group P4\textsubscript{2}/mnm in the entire composition range.  
Our results show that partial occupancy disrupts unit cell symmetry, potentially affecting the mechanical response of the \textsigma--phase.  
Using \textit{spglib*}~\cite{Spglib_Togo} as implemented in the \textit{atomistic simulation environment}~\cite{HjorthLarsen_2017}, we find that even before full optimization of the unit cell, the only decoration of the lattice  sites according to the partial occupancy breaks the tetragonal symmetry of the \textsigma phase.  
\MFmarginpar{*}{}{would it be possible that occupancy in 2a recovers symmetry somehow?}
Upon decoration, all the configurations acquire a triclinic unit cell with space group \textit{P1}. 
The cell vector lengths and angles of the optimized triclinic cells are shown in \autoref{fig:SymmetryConsiderations} for all the configurations.  
Although small, the introduced structural variations might affect the mechanical properties of the \textsigma-phase. 

\paragraph{Phase stability}\MFmarginpar{*}{}{include validation point}
The calculated convex hull for the full occupancy samples and both models with partial occupancy is shown in \autoref{subfig:FormationEnergies}. 
All formation enthalpies are referenced to the spin-polarized FM BCC ground states of Fe and Cr (Eq.~\eqref{eqn:formation_enthalpy}).
The formation enthalpies at 0\,K (DFT) of the \textsigma-phase are positive for all compositions, reflecting the metastable nature of the phase at low temperature. 
However, CEF results show that the \textsigma-phase becomes thermodynamically stable starting at approximately 873\,K, although the predicted stable composition appears shifted toward the Cr-rich region relative to experiment.
Introducing partial occupancy stabilizes the phase at the composition of interest: several PO samples fall below the tie-line of the full-occupancy convex hull.
In contrast, the FO configurations at the same nominal composition (FO1, FO2, FO3) lie tens of meV/atom above the convex-hull tie line.
No direct correlation between formation enthalpy and unit cell distortions is observed within the selected samples.

The formation enthalpies of the PO1 set approach the 0\,K Gibbs free energy from the CEF, even within our limited initial DFT selection; no individual PO configuration is found below this thermodynamic lower bound, as expected.
CEF site occupancies are reported alongside the experimental data from Yakel~\cite{yakel_atom_1983} in \autoref{subfig:CEF_Occupancy}.
The agreement with experiment is generally very good.
At 873\,K we obtain nearly full Fe occupancy in sites \siteA~and \siteD, both with 12-fold coordination, while experimental occupancy is approximately 10\% lower in both sites—the difference is compensated by a small excess of Fe in site \siteE.

Samples from the sublattice model retain the tetragonal symmetry of the \textsigma-phase unit cell after full optimization, as expected. 
The elastic tensor of a tetragonal unit cell can be reduced to 6 independent components.
In Voigt notation~\cite{golesorkhtabar_elastic_2013}:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:tetragonalElasticTensor}
  C^{tetragonal} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    C_{11} & C_{12} & C_{13} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    C_{12} & C_{11} & C_{13} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    C_{13} & C_{13} & C_{33} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & C_{44} & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & C_{44} & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & C_{66} \\
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

In the other hand, for the P1 space group of the PO1 and PO2 models, the elastic tensor has triclinic symmetry. 
Although such an elastic tensor would still be simmetric by definition, all the 21 components of the upper triangular half are in principle non-zero and independent from each other:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:triclinicElasticTensor}
  C^{triclinic} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    C_{11} & C_{12} & C_{13} & C_{14} & C_{15} & C_{16} \\
    C_{12} & C_{22} & C_{23} & C_{24} & C_{25} & C_{26} \\
    C_{13} & C_{23} & C_{33} & C_{34} & C_{35} & C_{36} \\
    C_{14} & C_{24} & C_{34} & C_{44} & C_{45} & C_{46} \\
    C_{15} & C_{25} & C_{35} & C_{45} & C_{55} & C_{56} \\
    C_{16} & C_{26} & C_{36} & C_{46} & C_{56} & C_{66} \\
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}


In consequence, 21 parametrized deformation should be applied to solve such an elastic tensor. 
In addition, for each deformation tensor enough parameters should be applied as to determine each of the elastic tensor components after a least squares fit.
This makes the determination of the elastic tensor of the non-symmetrical triclinic unit cell unfeasible in the whole configurational space.

However, the size of ad-hoc selected PO1 and PO2 plus the validation set is manageable with DFT calculations.

We quantify the accuracy of the sublattice approximations by measuring how their elastic constants deviate from the reference $C^{PO,\min}$ of the minimum-energy partial-occupancy configuration.
Three complementary metrics are defined.

For the tetragonal symmetry of the FO and CEF models, certain pairs of components are exactly equal, e.g.\ $C_{11}^{CEF} = C_{22}^{CEF}$.
The partial-occupancy samples break this equality.
To assess how much PO configurations violate tetragonal symmetry we define the ratio

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:ratios}
    r_{IJ,KL} = \dfrac{C^{PO}_{IJ}}{C^{PO}_{KL}}, \qquad C^{CEF}_{IJ} = C^{CEF}_{KL} 
\end{equation}

\noindent such that $r_{11,22}$, $r_{44,55}$ and $r_{13,23}$ are defined; a value of unity indicates tetragonal symmetry is preserved.

For the components that are non-zero in the tetragonal tensor, we define the relative deviation of any approximation $X \in \{FO,\,CEF\}$ from the minimum-energy PO reference:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:deviatiosnonzero}
\Delta^{\neq 0} _{IJ}(X) = \dfrac{C_{IJ}^{X} - C_{IJ}^{PO,\min}}{C_{IJ}^{PO,\min}}, \qquad C_{IJ}^{PO,\min} \neq 0
\end{equation}

Finally, for components that are zero in the tetragonal (FO and CEF) tensor but may be non-zero in the triclinic PO configuration, we report the absolute value of the PO component directly as a measure of symmetry-breaking:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:deviationsarezero}
\Delta^{= 0} _{IJ} = C^{PO}_{IJ}/\text{MPa}, \qquad C^{X}_{IJ} = 0,\; X\in\{FO,CEF\}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Minimum-energy PO as the best elastic constant estimate}

The central aim of this work is to quantify how well the classical sublattice approximations—the full-occupancy sublattice model (FO) and the compound energy formalism (CEF) average—reproduce the elastic properties of the Fe–Cr \textsigma-phase.
The minimum-energy partial-occupancy configuration $C^{PO,\min}$, identified by screening the full configurational space with the fitted GRACE potential and confirmed by DFT, serves as the physically best available estimate.
This is justified because $C^{PO,\min}$ represents the most thermodynamically stable explicit atomic arrangement within the PO constraint, which is closer to the true disordered ground state than either a single ordered FO end-member or a probability-weighted CEF average.

At the nominal Fe\textsubscript{16}Cr\textsubscript{14} composition, three FO configurations exist.
We identify the FO configuration with the lowest formation enthalpy (requiring three DFT single-point calculations), which we denote FO$_{\min}$, and compare its elastic constants to $C^{PO,\min}$.
We also evaluate the CEF elastic constants $c_{IJ}^{CEF}$ from Eq.~\eqref{cefcij} using the full set of 32 FO configurations, and compare those to $C^{PO,\min}$.

\paragraph{Computational workflow}

The combined DFT / MLIP workflow follows these steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(1)] Calculate formation energies and elastic constants for the 32 FO configurations and the FM BCC unary ground states.
    \item[(2)] Calculate energies and elastic constants for a selected subset of PO configurations, chosen to cover a representative sample of all sublattice chemical environments.
    \item[(3)] Train the GRACE potential on the collected DFT data: relaxation paths, random deformations, and nearest-neighbour distance expansions of BCC, FCC, and HCP unaries.
    \item[(4)] Use the fitted GRACE potential to rank the full PO configurational space by formation enthalpy and identify the lowest-energy configurations.
    \item[(5)] Compute the full elastic tensor with DFT for $C^{PO,\min}$, the minimum-energy PO configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

In this work we present a systematic study of the effects of partial sublattice occupancy on the elastic properties of the Fe–Cr \textsigma-phase.
For the first time, the elastic tensor of this phase is computed taking into account the experimentally observed partial occupancies, using a combined DFT and machine-learning interatomic potential workflow.
Formation enthalpies throughout are referenced to spin-polarized (FM) BCC Fe and Cr, providing a consistent energy scale.

The minimum-energy partial-occupancy configuration $C^{PO,\min}$—identified by screening the full configurational space with the fitted GRACE potential—serves as the physically most consistent estimate of the elastic tensor.
Comparing the traditional full-occupancy sublattice model (FO) and the compound energy formalism (CEF) to this reference, we find deviations of approximately 10\% in the dominant elastic tensor components.
Both classical approximations systematically overestimate stiffness relative to $C^{PO,\min}$.

Our results confirm that partial occupancy significantly influences the mechanical response of the \textsigma-phase in a way that cannot be captured by traditional full sublattice models.
The present workflow—DFT-anchored MLIP screening followed by targeted DFT elastic constant calculations for the minimum-energy configuration—provides a practical and computationally tractable path to include explicit configurational disorder in elastic property modeling of complex intermetallic compounds.
